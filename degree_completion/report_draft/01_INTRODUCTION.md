# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

The automatic analysis of persuasion in naturally occurring text has become an active topic
within natural language processing and computational argumentation [CITATION NEEDED — a general
reference for this field has not been verified against this project's reference list]. Unlike
curated corpora of formal debates or marketing copy, discussions between participants genuinely
trying to change, or resist changing, their minds offer a setting where persuasive success can
be observed directly rather than inferred after the fact.

Reddit's Change My View (CMV) subreddit is a widely used research setting for this purpose
(Priniski & Horne, 2018; Na & DeDeo, 2022). CMV asks a user to post an opinion they state they
are open to reconsidering; other users then reply with arguments intended to change that
opinion. The forum's distinguishing feature is the "delta" (∆) mechanism: if the original
poster is persuaded by a specific reply, they award that reply a delta — a naturally occurring,
per-comment label for persuasive success, generated by the person being persuaded rather than
inferred by an external annotator judging argument quality.

The delta label is useful, but it is important to be precise about what it does and does not
measure. A delta records that the original poster reported being persuaded by a particular
comment, under whatever conditions and incentives operate on the forum at that moment. It is an
operational proxy for persuasive success, not a verified or complete measurement of genuine,
lasting attitude change: it can be withheld for reasons unrelated to argument quality, and it
says nothing about whether the stated change persists outside the thread. Delta-award status is
treated throughout this project as the label the predictive task is built on, not as direct
evidence of an underlying psychological process.

A related motivation concerns what a predictive model built on this label can, and cannot, be
taken to show. Comments that receive deltas tend to differ from comments that do not along many
superficial textual dimensions, length being the most obvious. A model that performs well could
be recovering genuinely rhetorical information — how an argument is constructed — or simply
recovering comment length and its correlates, without engaging with argumentative structure at
all. These are very different findings, and this project's design increasingly centered on
separating them: distinguishing surface-level textual signals, principally comment length, from
signals tied to specific, nameable rhetorical strategies such as refutation or justification.

## 1.2 Research Problem and Objectives

The central predictive task addressed in this project is: given the text of a CMV comment, and
the original post it replies to, predict whether that comment will be awarded a delta. This is
framed as a per-comment binary classification problem, evaluated with a thread-disjoint split so
that a model cannot benefit from seeing a related comment from the same discussion during
training. The project's original objectives were to identify textual properties associated with
persuasion, including the role of comment length; to characterize differences between convincing
and non-convincing comments; to build a predictive model of persuasive success; and to test
whether a small set of explicitly defined rhetorical strategies adds predictive information
beyond simpler textual features and length, with external validation on an independently
constructed dataset.

The methodological challenge that came to shape the project's actual course of work is this:
comment length is strongly associated with persuasive success in this setting, to the point that
comment length alone can outperform considerably more elaborate feature pipelines. Predictive
performance on its own is therefore not sufficient evidence that a model has captured something
about rhetorical quality — it may simply be exploiting length. This was not fully anticipated in
the project's initial design; it emerged once the earliest predictive models were evaluated and
comment length was found to dominate every other feature tried. From that point, a substantial
part of the remaining work — the length-controlled comparisons and the subsequent strategy-level
ablation and interpretation analyses — was organized specifically around isolating what, if
anything, survives once length is statistically controlled for. This report presents that
development as the coherent line of inquiry it became, while being explicit that the
length-focused analyses were a response to an empirical finding made partway through the work,
not a design decided on in full detail in advance.

## 1.3 Research Questions

The project is organized around three research questions:

- **RQ1.** To what extent can persuasive success be predicted from textual features, and what
  role does comment length play?
- **RQ2.** Do rhetorical-strategy measures provide incremental predictive information beyond
  comment length and other textual features?
- **RQ3.** Which rhetorical strategies contribute to prediction, and how stable are these
  findings across datasets constructed using different methodologies?

These questions are addressed in sequence. RQ1 establishes the predictive task and the extent of
the length confound. RQ2 asks whether a defined rhetorical-strategy signal exists beyond that
confound. RQ3 asks which specific strategies carry that signal, and whether the answer
generalizes beyond the dataset on which it was found.

## 1.4 Research Approach and Contributions

To address these questions, a dataset of 3,971 comments drawn from CMV threads was constructed
for this project, with 641 comments (16.1%) labeled as persuasive based on delta awards. A range
of engineered textual features was computed per comment — sentiment, tone, rhetorical-appeal,
readability, and evidence-marker measures, several derived using large-language-model scoring
where lexicon- and classifier-based methods proved unsuitable — alongside baseline classifiers
trained with a thread-grouped, leakage-free evaluation split.

Initial modeling showed that comment length alone was a stronger predictor than the full
engineered-feature pipeline, motivating a dedicated length analysis: a direct comparison of word
count between convincing and non-convincing comments, and length-controlled comparisons in which
comments were resampled so that length no longer differed systematically between classes. These
comparisons indicate that a nonzero predictive signal survives length control, though
substantially diminished relative to the signal observed without it — evidence of additional
information beyond length, not proof of complete independence from it.

To characterize that additional signal directly, eight rhetorical strategies relevant to CMV
argumentation — including refutation, justification, concession, and evidence use — were defined
and scored on a numeric rubric by a large language model, following the general scoring
methodology introduced by Labruna et al. (2026) for a different taxonomy and domain. These scores
were added to the engineered-feature model in an ablation analysis, and their individual
contributions were examined using SHAP-based model interpretation and model-free rank-biserial
effect sizes, on both the full population and a length-matched subsample. Refutation is the
strategy whose model-based (SHAP) importance is most robust to length control; justification
shows the largest univariate (rank-biserial) effect size overall. These are different importance
measures that need not agree, and both are reported rather than collapsed into one ranking.

Finally, the same rubric, and the model architecture and training configuration already
selected on this project's own data, were applied to Winning Arguments, an independently
constructed persuasion dataset built by other researchers using a different sampling methodology
(Tan et al., 2016). A new model instance was fit on Winning Arguments' own training split and
evaluated on its own held-out split — a transfer of the selected methodology, not a zero-shot
application of the original fitted model. The rhetorical-strategy scores again carried predictive
information beyond length, supporting the general claim that such information exists. However,
which specific strategy dominates differs between the two datasets, and because no comparable
length-controlled ablation was performed on Winning Arguments, this result supports a
strategy-level signal on a second dataset, not evidence that its independence from length
replicates to the same degree.

This project's contributions are accordingly modest and empirical rather than methodological
novelties in their own right: a reproducible pipeline for isolating and quantifying a length
confound in a persuasion-prediction task; an adaptation of LLM-based rhetorical-strategy scoring
to a new taxonomy purpose-built for CMV, with an explicit, if limited, reliability check; and a
partial cross-corpus validation that distinguishes, honestly, what replicates from what does not.

## 1.5 Report Organization

The remainder of this report follows the structure outlined in `REPORT_OUTLINE.md`. The Methods
chapter describes data collection and annotation, feature engineering, model training and
evaluation methodology, the baseline investigation that surfaced the length confound, the
length-controlled analysis, rhetorical-strategy scoring and its validation, the strategy
ablation, strategy-level interpretation, and the external validation on Winning Arguments. The
Results chapter presents the corresponding quantitative findings in the same order. The
Discussion chapter synthesizes these findings into answers to the three research questions,
situates them against related prior work on CMV, and states the project's limitations —
including the reliability of LLM-scored features, the single-platform and single-language scope
of the dataset, and how far point estimates here should be read as suggestive rather than
statistically decisive — together with directions for future work.
